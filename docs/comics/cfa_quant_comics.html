1
Rates and Returns
Interest rates, HPR, Arithmetic vs Geometric, real vs nominal — the return zoo.
What's an Interest Rate, Anyway?
"Same rate, three interpretations! Context decides which hat it's wearing."
r = required return = discount rate = opportunity cost. It's ALL of these at once.
Building Blocks of Interest Rate
Real Risk-Free Rate 🏦
+
Inflation Premium 📈
+
Risk Premiums (default, liquidity, maturity) ⚠️
r = Real risk-free rate + Inflation premium + Default + Liquidity + Maturity premiums.
Holding Period Return (HPR)
HPR = (P₁ − P₀ + Income) / P₀
"Buy at 100, sell at 110, get $5 income → HPR = 15%!"
HPR = total gain (price + income) divided by the initial price. Simple. Powerful.
Arithmetic vs Geometric Mean Return — The Big Fight!
ARITHMETIC
✅ Best for: forward-looking estimates
⚠️ Overstates compounded growth!
VS
GEOMETRIC
✅ Best for: past performance
✅ Accounts for compounding!
"Returns: +50%, −50%. Arithmetic = 0%. Geometric = −13.4%! The geometric truth HURTS. 😅"
Geometric mean ≤ Arithmetic mean. Always use geometric for multi-period compounded performance.
Money-Weighted vs Time-Weighted
"TWR = gold standard for comparing fund managers. MWR = actual investor experience."
TWR eliminates the effect of external cash flows — best for evaluating manager skill.
Real vs Nominal Returns
(1+nominal) = (1+real)(1+inflation)
Real return removes inflation. It's what actually grows your purchasing power.
📈

Module 1 Key Takeaway

Interest rates have three roles (return, discount rate, opportunity cost) and are built from real rate + risk premiums. Use geometric mean for compounded past performance, TWR to compare managers, and always strip inflation to find the real return.

2
Time Value of Money in Finance
Bonds, equities, forward rates, no-arbitrage, option pricing — TVM everywhere!
Bond = PV of Future Cash Flows
Price = Σ C/(1+r)ᵗ + Face/(1+r)ⁿ
"When rates ↑, price ↓. Bond price and yield move OPPOSITE!"
A bond's price = PV of all coupons + PV of face value, discounted at the market yield.
Equity: Gordon Growth Model
V₀ = D₁ / (r − g)
"Stock value = next dividend divided by (required return − growth). Simple!"
Gordon Growth Model: stock = PV of perpetually growing dividends. Works when g < r.
No-Arbitrage Forward Rates
"Both paths must give same result — or arbitrageurs will exploit the difference!"
Forward rates are implied by spot rates via no-arbitrage: (1+S₂)² = (1+S₁)(1+₁f₁).
Option Pricing via Cash Flow Additivity (No-Arbitrage)
KEY IDEA
Put-Call Parity:
C + PV(X) = P + S
Two portfolios with identical future payoffs must have identical prices today — or free money exists!
"If left side ≠ right side → BUY the cheap portfolio, SELL the expensive one. Riskless profit! But markets eliminate these quickly."
Cash flow additivity + no-arbitrage → price any instrument by matching future payoffs.
💵

Module 2 Key Takeaway

TVM is the engine of finance. Bond prices = PV of cash flows (inverse to yield). Stocks = PV of growing dividends. Forward rates are implied by spot rates via no-arbitrage. Put-call parity prices options. One principle rules them all: same future cash flows → same present price.

3
Statistical Measures of Asset Returns
Mean, median, variance, skewness, kurtosis, correlation — stats boot camp!
Mean, Median, Mode — Who Wins?
"In right-skewed data: Mode < Median < Mean. The mean gets dragged by outliers!"
Skewness pulls the mean away from median. Use median for outlier-prone financial data.
Measuring Spread: Variance & Std Dev
Range = Max − Min  📏
Variance = avg squared deviations from mean  σ²
Std Dev = √Variance  σ  ← same units as returns!
CV = σ / Mean  ← risk per unit of return
Std Dev = most common risk measure. CV lets you compare risk across different assets.
Skewness & Kurtosis — Shape Matters!
"Fat tails = extreme outcomes more likely than normal. BIG deal in risk management!"
Excess kurtosis > 0 = fat tails. Financial returns often leptokurtic — crashes happen more than normal predicts!
Correlation — How Two Assets Move Together
"Correlation ranges −1 to +1. NEVER implies causation! Two ice cream stocks may correlate — but eating ice cream doesn't cause the market to rise. 🍦"
Correlation ρ ∈ [−1, +1]. Key for diversification — combining low-corr assets reduces portfolio risk.
📊

Module 3 Key Takeaway

Central tendency (mean/median/mode), dispersion (variance, std dev, CV), shape (skewness, kurtosis), and correlation form the statistical toolkit for analysing returns. Fat tails and skewness mean normal distribution assumptions can be dangerous in real markets!

Probability Trees & Conditional Expectations
Expected value, probability trees, Bayes' theorem — probability in action!
Expected Value — Probability-Weighted Average
"E[X] = probability-weighted sum of all possible outcomes. The long-run average!"
Expected value = Σ P(xᵢ)·xᵢ. It's the fair price and the long-run average of repeated trials.
Probability Tree — Map All Scenarios
Trees map conditional outcomes. E[X] = multiply joint probabilities × outcomes, sum all branches.
Bayes' Theorem — Updating Beliefs with New Info
PRIOR BELIEF
+
NEW DATA
LIKELIHOOD
=
POSTERIOR
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)   ← Bayes' Theorem
🌳

Module 4 Key Takeaway

Expected value weights outcomes by probability. Probability trees visualise conditional paths. Bayes' theorem is the engine for updating beliefs: prior × likelihood = posterior. Essential for scenario analysis and forecasting in investments.

5
Portfolio Mathematics
Expected return, variance, covariance, correlation, normal distribution & VaR.
Portfolio Expected Return
"Just the weighted average of each asset's return. Nice and clean!"
Portfolio E[R] = Σ wᵢ · E[Rᵢ]. Simple weighted average. The magic is in the variance.
Diversification Magic 🪄
"When ρ = −1 → variance drops to ZERO! That's perfect diversification."
σ²ₚ = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂σ₁σ₂ρ₁₂
Normal Distribution & VaR
"VaR at 5% = I'll lose MORE than this only 5% of the time. Regulatory gold standard!"
Normal distribution: 68-95-99.7 rule. VaR answers "what's the worst likely loss at X% confidence?"
🗂️

Module 5 Key Takeaway

Portfolio return = weighted average of asset returns. Portfolio variance includes a covariance term — this is where diversification lives! Low/negative correlation reduces portfolio risk without sacrificing return. Normal distribution assumptions underpin VaR and risk budgeting.

6
Simulation Methods
Lognormal distribution, Monte Carlo simulation, Bootstrapping.
Why Asset Prices Are Lognormal
"If returns are normal → prices are lognormal. And a stock can never be worth less than $0!"
Lognormal prices = naturally bounded above zero. ln(price) is normally distributed.
Monte Carlo Simulation 🎰
"Run 10,000 random scenarios. Count outcomes. Build a distribution of possibilities!"
Monte Carlo: simulate many random paths using assumed distributions → estimate risk & return distributions.
Bootstrapping — Resample the Data!
Bootstrapping resamples with replacement from actual data — makes no parametric assumptions.
🎲

Module 6 Key Takeaway

Asset prices follow lognormal distribution (bounded at zero, right-skewed). Monte Carlo simulates thousands of paths under assumed distributions. Bootstrapping resamples actual historical data — model-free and assumption-free. Both are powerful tools for estimating risk.

Estimation and Inference
Sampling methods, the magical Central Limit Theorem, standard error.
4 Ways to Sample a Population
①

②

③
Cluster — random clusters, sample whole cluster 🔵
④

Choose sampling method carefully — bias in sampling means bias in conclusions.
The Central Limit Theorem — One of Statistics' Greatest Hits! 🎸
ANY Population
→
large n samples
Sample Means
"No matter how weird the underlying distribution — sample means become NORMAL as n grows. This is why statistics WORKS in finance!"
SE = σ/√n  ← standard error of mean
CLT: sample means → Normal(μ, σ²/n) as n→∞. Standard error shrinks as sample grows.
🔬

Module 7 Key Takeaway

Sampling method choice affects data quality. The Central Limit Theorem is the cornerstone of inferential statistics: sample means are approximately normal for large n, regardless of population shape. Standard error = σ/√n — bigger samples → more precise estimates.

8
Hypothesis Testing
H₀ vs H₁, p-values, t-tests, Type I & II errors — prove it statistically!
The 5-Step Hypothesis Testing Process
① State H₀ (null) and Hₐ (alternative)
▼
② Choose significance level α (1%, 5%, 10%)
▼
③ Calculate test statistic (t, z, F, χ²)
▼
④ Find critical value / p-value
▼
⑤ Reject H₀ or Fail to Reject H₀ ✅
Always: state → choose α → compute statistic → compare to critical value → decide.
Type I & Type II Errors — The Double Danger
Type I (α): reject true H₀. Type II (β): fail to reject false H₀. Reducing α increases β!
The p-value — Smallest α to Reject H₀
"p-value = probability of seeing this result IF H₀ were true. Small p = suspicious of H₀!"
p-value < α → reject H₀. p-value is NOT the probability that H₀ is true — common mistake!
Which Test to Use? The Quick Reference
t-test
Test a mean or difference between means. Unknown σ. Use t-distribution.
z-test
Test a mean when σ is known OR n is large (CLT kicks in).
F-test
Compare two variances. Always right-tailed. Ratio of two χ² distributions.
χ² test
Test a single variance or goodness of fit. Always positive, right-skewed.
Match the right test to the right question: means → t/z, variances → F or χ², always check assumptions!
🔍

Module 8 Key Takeaway

Hypothesis testing has 5 steps. p-value < α → reject H₀. Type I error (false positive) = α. Type II error (false negative) = β. There's always a trade-off: tightening one loosens the other. Use t for means, F for comparing variances, χ² for single variance.

Parametric & Non-Parametric Tests of Independence
Correlation tests, Spearman rank correlation, contingency tables.
Is That Correlation Real or Random?
"We found r = 0.6 — but is it statistically significant? Use the t-test for correlation!"
Parametric test: t = r√(n−2) / √(1−r²). Reject H₀: ρ=0 if |t| exceeds critical value.
Spearman Rank Correlation — No Assumptions!
"Convert data to ranks first, then compute correlation. Works for non-normal data!"
Spearman = nonparametric. Ranks the data, then correlates ranks. Robust to outliers and non-normality.
Chi-Square Test of Independence
"Are stock direction and economic cycle independent? Chi-square tells you!"
Contingency table + χ² test checks independence between two categorical variables. No normality needed.
📐

Module 9 Key Takeaway

Parametric correlation test uses t-distribution (assumes normality). Spearman rank is nonparametric — converts data to ranks, robust to outliers. Chi-square contingency table tests independence of categorical variables. Always match the test to the data type!

10
Simple Linear Regression
OLS estimation, 4 key assumptions, ANOVA, R², prediction intervals.
The Regression Line: Best Fit Through the Data
"OLS minimizes the SUM of SQUARED residuals — least squares!"
Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X. OLS finds b₀, b₁ that minimise Σεᵢ². Slope b₁ = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X).
The 4 Assumptions of OLS — Violate These at Your Peril!
① Linearity
X and Y have a linear relationship. Plot it first!
② Homoskedasticity
Variance of errors is CONSTANT. If not → heteroskedasticity → wrong SEs!
③ Independence
Errors not correlated with each other. Violation = serial correlation!
④ Normality
Errors normally distributed → enables valid t-tests and p-values!
If any assumption is violated, OLS estimates may be BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased) but inference is invalid.
ANOVA & R² — How Good Is Your Model?
"R² = 0.85 means X explains 85% of Y's variation. Higher = better fit!"
R² = SSR/SST. ANOVA F-test checks if the overall model is statistically significant.
Log-Lin, Lin-Log, Log-Log — Pick Your Form!
Lin-Lin: Y = b₀+b₁X  ← standard, both untransformed
Log-Lin: ln(Y) = b₀+b₁X  ← % change in Y for unit ΔX
Lin-Log: Y = b₀+b₁ln(X)  ← unit ΔY for % change in X
Log-Log: ln(Y)=b₀+b₁ln(X)  ← b₁ is elasticity!
Choose functional form based on the economic relationship. R² and residual plots help decide.
📉

Module 10 Key Takeaway

Simple linear regression: Ŷ = b₀ + b₁X, fit by OLS (minimise squared residuals). Four assumptions (linearity, homoskedasticity, independence, normality). R² measures fit. ANOVA F-test checks overall significance. Log transformations handle nonlinear and percentage relationships.
